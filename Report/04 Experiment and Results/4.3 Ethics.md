# 4.3 Ethics

↑ [[04 Experiment and Results]]

## 4.3.1 Ethical Considerations in Driver Monitoring Systems

Driver Monitoring Systems (DMS) handle sensitive biometric and behavioural data inside a private vehicle environment. Therefore, ethical design principles must be integrated into the system architecture from the beginning to ensure user trust, safety, and responsible operation.

### 4.3.1.1 Privacy Protection

The proposed DMS prioritizes driver privacy by processing all video frames, facial landmarks, eye-gaze information, and heart rate data locally on the Raspberry Pi device. No personal or biometric data is stored permanently or transmitted over external networks such as Wi-Fi or Bluetooth. This edge-processing approach minimizes privacy risks and prevents unauthorized access to sensitive information.

### 4.3.1.2 Data Security

System security is enhanced by avoiding cloud connectivity and keeping all processing within the embedded hardware platform. Communication between components such as sensors, cameras, and processing modules uses secure hardware-level interfaces (e.g., I2C and CSI), reducing exposure to external cyber threats and data breaches.

### 4.3.1.3 User Consent and Transparency

The DMS operates only with the driver's awareness and consent. Drivers should be informed about the type of collected data, the purpose of monitoring, and how the system uses this information to improve road safety. Users must also retain the ability to disable monitoring features whenever desired.

## 4.3.2 Fairness, Comfort, and Responsibility

### 4.3.2.1 Fairness

The AI models used in the DMS must be evaluated across diverse demographic groups to ensure consistent and unbiased performance regardless of age, gender, skin tone, or facial characteristics. Ethical AI development requires minimizing bias in training datasets to avoid unequal system accuracy among users.

### 4.3.2.2 Driver Comfort

Alert mechanisms should be carefully calibrated to avoid excessive warnings, stress, or driver distraction. Frequent false alarms may reduce driver trust in the system and negatively impact overall safety. Therefore, the balance between sensitivity and usability is an important ethical consideration.

### 4.3.2.3 Responsibility and System Limitations

The DMS is designed as a driver assistance tool rather than a fully autonomous safety system. Drivers and operators must be informed about situations where system performance may decrease, such as poor lighting conditions or improper sensor placement. Responsibility for safe vehicle operation remains with the driver at all times.

### 4.3.2.4 Continuous Validation

Ethical responsibility continues after deployment. The system should undergo ongoing testing and performance evaluation in real-world conditions to ensure reliability and maintain safety standards over time.

> [!warning] Note the tension against [[3.3 Dataset Selection and Collection|the custom-data plan]]: fairness (§4.3.2.1) requires diverse training data across age/gender/eyewear, which is exactly why 10–20 varied volunteers are specified there.
